Is it illegal to buy online reviews?

Last reviewed 30 July 2026

Yes. Buying fake consumer reviews is unlawful in the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Union and Australia. In the US it breaches the FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, with civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. In the UK the CMA can fine a business up to 10% of its annual global turnover without going to court. In the EU it is a blacklisted practice, prohibited in all circumstances.

Liability falls on the business that commissions the reviews, not only on the person who writes them.

United States: FTC Rule 16 CFR Part 465

The Federal Trade Commission's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials took effect on 21 October 2024. It moved fake reviews out of the realm of general "unfair and deceptive practices" enforcement and into a specific trade regulation rule — which matters, because a rule violation authorises courts to impose civil penalties directly.

The maximum civil penalty for a knowing violation is $53,088, a figure adjusted for inflation each January. It rose from $51,744 to $53,088 with effect from 17 January 2025 and was not adjusted for 2026.

The rule is broader than most buyers assume. Alongside buying and selling fake reviews, it also prohibits:

  • Review gating — soliciting reviews only from customers you expect to be positive. No review here is fake, and it is still a violation.
  • Insider reviews without clear disclosure of the connection — including reviews written by owners, employees, family members or agencies.
  • Suppressing negative reviews on your own site or channel.
  • Buying followers or engagement to misrepresent influence.

"Per violation" is the important phrase

The penalty ceiling is not per business or per incident. A course of conduct involving many reviews can be treated as many violations. A package of twenty-five purchased reviews does not attract one $53,088 cap — the theoretical exposure scales with the volume bought. This is why the arithmetic on a $125 review package is so poor: the downside is not proportionate to the spend.

United Kingdom: the DMCC Act 2024

The consumer protection provisions of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 came into force on 6 April 2025, and they changed UK enforcement in two significant ways.

First, fake reviews are now expressly banned — including commissioning them, hosting them without taking reasonable steps to check authenticity, and misrepresenting an aggregate rating that incorporates them.

Second, and more consequentially, the Competition and Markets Authority can now decide that a business has broken consumer law and impose a fine directly, without first going to court. Previously the CMA had to litigate. Now it does not.

The ceiling is 10% of annual global turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher. The CMA has been running a sweep of review platforms to identify non-compliance, and an initial three-month grace period aimed at helping businesses achieve compliance has long since expired.

European Union: blacklisted, in all circumstances

The Omnibus Directive amended the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive to add two items to Annex I — the EU's blacklist of practices banned outright:

  • Item 23b: stating that reviews are submitted by genuine consumers without taking reasonable and proportionate steps to verify that.
  • Item 23c: submitting, or commissioning another person to submit, false reviews or endorsements, or misrepresenting consumer reviews in order to promote a product.

Annex I practices are prohibited per se. There is no case-by-case assessment of whether consumers were actually harmed, no materiality threshold, and no defence based on scale or intent. Penalties are set by each member state and vary considerably.

Criminal exposure: it has already happened

In September 2018 a criminal court in Lecce, Italy convicted the operator of PromoSalento, a business that sold fake reviews to hotels and restaurants. The court held that writing fake online reviews under a false identity constituted criminal conduct under Italian law. The sentence was nine months' imprisonment plus €8,000 in costs and damages. It was the first conviction for review fraud anywhere in the world.

Two details from that case are worth noting. Tripadvisor supported the prosecution with information from its in-house fraud team and its legal counsel — platforms cooperate actively with authorities. And PromoSalento was traced despite routinely rotating usernames and email addresses to evade detection.

"I only bought them — I didn't write them"

This is the most common misconception, and it is wrong in every jurisdiction above. The rules target commissioning fake reviews. EU Annex I item 23c names it explicitly. The FTC rule covers buying as well as creating.

In practice the buyer is often the easier enforcement target than the seller. A business is identifiable, locally regulated, holds assets and has a reputation to lose. The seller is frequently an offshore operation using rotating payment channels and disposable domains, effectively beyond the reach of a regulator. When a review-selling network is dismantled, its customer list is a list of identifiable, reachable, liable businesses.

The seller bears none of this risk. The regulatory exposure, the platform penalties and the reputational damage attach to your business. The seller has already been paid, holds a record of the transaction and your business link, and faces no consequence if your reviews are removed.
