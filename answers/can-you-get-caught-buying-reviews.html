Can you get caught buying reviews?

Last reviewed 30 July 2026

Yes, and routinely. Tripadvisor alone identified more than 1.3 million fake review submissions in a single year and applied ranking penalties to more than 33,000 businesses. Detection is mostly retroactive — reviews that pass initial screening are commonly removed weeks or months later, once the seller's wider network is mapped.

The critical point most buyers miss: you are not assessed on your own reviews alone. You are assessed on the entire footprint of the seller you bought from.

Detection looks at the network, not the review

A well-written five-star review is not hard to produce, and platforms stopped relying on the text of a review as the primary signal a long time ago. What they analyse is the surrounding structure.

  • Account and device reuse. Review sellers operate a finite pool of accounts, and they resell that pool to every customer. The same accounts therefore appear across dozens or hundreds of unrelated businesses in unrelated categories and countries — a pattern that has no innocent explanation.
  • Baseline anomalies. A business averaging two reviews a month that suddenly receives twenty-five is flagged by its own history. Drip-feeding reduces the spike but does not remove the underlying signal, because the reviewers still don't behave like your actual customers.
  • Reviewer-to-business mismatch. An account that reviews a dentist in Ohio, a hotel in Puglia and a moving company in Manchester within one week is not a customer of any of them.
  • Payment and infrastructure links. Shared IP ranges, hosting, contact details and payment rails tie a seller's operations together, and by extension their customers.

Why this is the real problem with buying

Because the accounts are shared, your exposure is determined by the seller's least careful customer, not by your own caution. When any one client of that seller triggers investigation, the account cluster is identified and every business it touched is surfaced at once. You have no visibility into who else is buying from the same pool, and no ability to manage that risk.

This is precisely how the PromoSalento case unravelled. The operator rotated usernames and email addresses specifically to avoid scrutiny, and Tripadvisor still traced the accounts back to the source — then supported a criminal prosecution that ended in a nine-month prison sentence.

"Non-drop" and "sticky" guarantees cannot be honoured

Nearly every review seller advertises some version of a permanence guarantee — non-drop, sticky, replacement warranty, "our advanced methods ensure reviews won't be flagged." Consider what that promise requires the seller to control.

It requires them to know, in advance, how a platform's detection models will evolve. Those models are retrained continuously and applied retrospectively to content already published. A review that survives its first week tells you nothing about whether it survives its sixth month. No seller can make a forward-looking guarantee about a system they do not operate and cannot observe.

The guarantee is also commercially worthless. Enforcing it would mean pursuing a claim founded on a contract to commit an unlawful act — against a counterparty who is typically offshore, anonymous and paid in crypto or wallet transfers. In practice the "guarantee" means the seller offers to sell you replacements.

The claimWhat it actually means
"Non-drop / sticky reviews"The seller cannot see or influence platform enforcement. This is a prediction dressed as a warranty.
"Aged accounts, residential IPs"Those same aged accounts are being sold to every other customer, which is what makes the cluster detectable.
"Drip-feed delivery"Smooths the volume spike. Does not address account reuse, reviewer-business mismatch, or network analysis.
"100% success rate guaranteed"Measured at delivery, not at survival. Reviews appearing on your profile is not the same as reviews staying there.
"Free replacements if removed"Replacements come from the same compromised account pool, and each round adds to the evidence trail against your listing.

Getting caught once can mean getting caught everywhere

Since 2023, the major review platforms have shared fraud intelligence with one another through the Coalition for Trusted Reviews. Members include Amazon, Booking.com, Expedia Group, Glassdoor, Tripadvisor and Trustpilot. One of the Coalition's four stated areas of focus is bad actor intelligence — sharing information on how fraudulent operators work, including companies selling fake reviews to businesses.

The practical consequence for a buyer: the seller network you transact with is likely already known to platforms you have never dealt with. A business operating across several platforms is not taking one isolated risk per platform. It is taking one risk that can surface on all of them.

Detection is not the slow part — enforcement is

Buyers often read the absence of an immediate consequence as evidence they got away with it. Usually it means the removal and the penalty are still in the queue.

Tripadvisor's ranking penalties run for 365 days from the date of removal, decaying incrementally each day until they expire on day 366. That clock starts when the fraud is actioned, which may be many months after the reviews were bought — and long after the seller has been paid and become uncontactable.
