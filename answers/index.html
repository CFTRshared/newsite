Buying Online Reviews: The Facts

Awareness initiative. This section is published by the Coalition for Trusted Reviews, whose members include Amazon, Booking.com, Expedia Group, G2, HolidayCheck, Tripadvisor and Trustpilot. This domain also hosts a simulated review-selling storefront, used to warn prospective buyers at the point of purchase. No reviews are sold here and no payments are accepted.

What actually happens when a business buys online reviews

Buying reviews is unlawful in the United States, the United Kingdom and the European Union, and has produced a criminal conviction and a prison sentence in Italy. Detection is retroactive, so purchased reviews are routinely removed long after payment — and the penalties land on the business that bought them, not on the seller.

If you have already paid someone, start here.

Most people who go looking for a review seller are not career fraudsters. They are business owners with too few reviews, a competitor whose rating looks implausible, and no obvious way to catch up. The sales pitch is built for exactly that position: fast, cheap, low-risk, everyone does it.

The pitch is wrong about the risk. What follows is what the record actually shows, and all of it is sourced.

Start with your question

  • Is it illegal to buy online reviews?

    Yes — in the US, UK, EU and Australia. The rules, the fine ceilings, and why “I only bought them, I didn’t write them” is not a defence.

  • Can you actually get caught?

    Yes, routinely. Why the assurances a seller gives you cannot be verified, why detection is retroactive, and why getting caught on one platform can mean getting caught on all of them.

  • What are the penalties?

    Reviews removed, ranking penalties, awards eligibility gone, a public warning badge on your own listing, profile suspension, and regulatory fines on top.

    Why paying never ends it, why there is almost no recourse, and the steps that limit the damage — including why self-reporting protects you.

What to do instead

The underlying problem — not enough reviews — has legitimate solutions. They are slower, and the reviews they earn you are legitimate, defensible and far less likely to be removed.

  • Ask every customer, every time. Most satisfied customers will leave a review if you ask directly and make the process easy. Very few will do it unprompted. This one change can close most review-count gaps.
  • Use the platform’s own tools. Most major platforms provide free review-request links, QR codes and email templates. Reviews collected that way are at much less risk of removal.
  • Ask everyone, not just the happy ones. Filtering requests to customers you expect to be positive is itself a violation, even though no review is fake.
  • Never offer anything in exchange — no discounts, prize draws, free products or refunds — unless the platform explicitly permits it and the incentive is disclosed.
  • Report the competitor you’re trying to catch up with. If a rival’s rating looks implausible, report it. Platforms act on these reports, and it addresses the actual problem rather than adding to it.

Reporting review fraud

If you have been approached by a review seller, threatened by one, or believe a competitor is buying reviews, report it to the platform concerned. Every Coalition member operates a content-integrity team that acts on these reports, and members share intelligence on fraudulent operators with one another.
