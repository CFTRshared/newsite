What happens if you buy fake reviews?

Last reviewed 30 July 2026

The consequences arrive in sequence, not all at once, and typically long after the seller has been paid. Reviews are removed. The listing takes a ranking penalty — on Tripadvisor, one that runs for 365 days from the date of removal. Awards eligibility goes. In severe cases a public warning badge appears on the listing where your customers can see it. A Google Business Profile can be suspended or permanently removed. Regulators can then impose civil penalties independently.

Every item in that sequence attaches to your business. None of it attaches to the seller.

Stage 1 — The reviews are removed

This is the outcome buyers treat as the worst case. It is the mildest one, and it is close to inevitable, because detection is applied retrospectively to content already published.

The important asymmetry is the timing. The reviews are removed months after you paid; the seller became uncontactable within days. You have paid for an asset with no residual value and retained the full liability that came with it.

Stage 2 — Ranking penalties

Removal alone would leave you roughly where you started. Platforms don't stop at removal, because that would make buying reviews a free option.

Tripadvisor applies a ranking penalty to the listing: a review identified as violating its fraud guidelines can trigger a penalty lasting 365 days from the date of removal, decaying incrementally each day until it expires on day 366. During that year the listing is positioned below where its genuine reviews would place it.

The scale is not marginal. In a single year Tripadvisor applied ranking penalties to more than 33,000 businesses for fraud.

The net effect on visibility is negative, not neutral

A business that buys reviews and is caught does not return to its starting position. It ends up below it — same genuine review count as before, minus a year of suppressed ranking, having paid for the privilege.

Stage 3 — Award and recognition disqualification

Businesses found to have engaged in review fraud can be disqualified from platform awards and recognition programmes. For hospitality and travel businesses these badges often drive meaningful booking volume, and losing eligibility is a direct revenue effect that is invisible in any comparison of package prices.

Stage 4 — Public warning badges

This is the consequence buyers are least prepared for, because it is visible to customers rather than only to the business.

For the most severe cases Tripadvisor applies a red badge — a warning notice displayed on the business's own page telling travellers that a pattern of suspicious review activity has been identified. Every red badge is enforced together with a ranking penalty. In one year Tripadvisor issued 341 of them.

A red badge inverts the entire purpose of the purchase. The business bought reviews to look more trustworthy, and ended up with a notice on its listing telling prospective customers that its reviews cannot be trusted. It also creates a permanent, screenshot-able artefact that competitors, journalists and customers can circulate.

Stage 5 — Profile suspension

On some platforms the listing itself is at risk. Buying reviews, posting fake reviews or improperly incentivising customers violates Google's policies and can result in removals, listing restrictions, suspension, or permanent removal for repeated violations.

For a local business dependent on Search and Maps, a suspension is not a ranking problem — it is the disappearance of the primary discovery channel. Practitioners who handle reinstatements report call volumes dropping by as much as 90% during a suspension. Reinstatement is not guaranteed, and businesses frequently lose their accumulated legitimate reviews and ranking history in the process.

Marketplace sellers face the equivalent exposure: Amazon has pursued legal action against fake review brokers, including against the administrators of more than 10,000 Facebook groups organising review fraud, and selling-account suspension is a standard consequence for participating sellers.

Stage 6 — Regulatory penalties

Platform enforcement and regulatory enforcement are separate tracks, and one does not substitute for the other. A single course of conduct can produce both.

JurisdictionRegulatorMaximum exposure
United StatesFTC — 16 CFR Part 465Up to $53,088 per violation, and a course of conduct involving many reviews can constitute many violations
United KingdomCMA — DMCC Act 202410% of annual global turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher — imposed directly, without going to court
European UnionNational consumer authorities — UCPD Annex IBlacklisted practice, prohibited in all circumstances; penalties set per member state
ItalyCriminal courtsPrecedent exists: nine months' imprisonment plus €8,000 in costs and damages

The arithmetic

Review sellers price in the range of a few dollars per review — a typical package sits between $25 and $150. Set that against the exposure above.

A $125 purchase can produce: the loss of the $125; removal of everything bought; a 365-day ranking penalty; disqualification from awards; a public warning badge on your listing; suspension of a Google Business Profile that may take your genuine reviews with it; and regulatory exposure that scales with the number of reviews you bought.

And the seller retains your business link, your email address, your payment record, and documentary proof that you commissioned an unlawful act. What happens next is covered on the following page.
