I paid for reviews and got scammed. What now?

Last reviewed 30 July 2026

Assume the money is gone — it almost certainly is, and chasing it will cost you more than it recovers. The urgent issue is different: the seller now holds your business details, your payment record and proof that you commissioned an unlawful act. That is leverage, and a meaningful share of sellers use it.

The two things that matter most: stop paying, and self-report to the platform before the seller does. Both are explained below.

If you are reading this, the likeliest situation is one of four. The reviews never arrived. They arrived and were removed. The seller kept escalating — a delivery fee, an unlocking fee, a premium tier — and each payment produced another demand. Or the seller has started threatening you.

All four are standard. None of them mean you were unlucky with an otherwise legitimate vendor. They are the business model.

The extortion pattern

Once a seller holds your business link and evidence you paid, the incentives change. You are no longer a customer to retain — you are a target with a demonstrated willingness to pay and a strong reason not to complain. The recognised patterns:

  • Escalating fees. Each payment unlocks a new prerequisite. There is no completion point, because completion isn't the product.
  • Threatened exposure. The seller offers to report you to the platform, or to your regulator, unless you keep paying.
  • Negative review attacks. The same account pool that was going to praise you is turned against you. Review extortion is a well-documented industry in its own right: more than 150 businesses across the US have been targeted with campaigns of fake negative reviews followed by demands for payment, commonly around $100 per review to remove them.

Paying does not stop it. This is the most consistent finding in reporting on review extortion. One business owner paid $250 in removal fees to operators traced to Pakistan and Bangladesh — and more negative reviews simply appeared. Businesses that come through review extortion intact almost never pay, because paying identifies you as someone who will pay again.

Why you have almost no recourse

Three obstacles compound, and it is worth understanding them so you stop spending effort on the wrong thing.

The contract is unenforceable. You cannot sue on an agreement to commit an unlawful act. There is no forum in which "I paid for fake reviews and they weren't delivered" is a claim.

The payment rails were chosen to prevent reversal. Sellers ask for cryptocurrency and wallet transfers for exactly this reason. Card payments occasionally leave room for a chargeback, but the merchant descriptor is usually a shell and the dispute requires describing what you bought.

Complaining means disclosing. The seller's real protection isn't anonymity — it's that reporting them means reporting yourself. That is the trap, and it is deliberate. It is also, as set out below, less of a trap than it appears.

What to do, in order

1. Stop paying, and stop negotiating

Do not send another payment, and do not enter into discussion about a smaller amount. Every reply confirms the channel is live and the target is responsive. Cease contact.

2. Preserve everything

Before you delete anything: screenshot the full message history, the site you bought from, the pricing page, the payment confirmations, transaction hashes or references, and any threats. Do this now, because seller infrastructure is deliberately short-lived. This evidence is what supports both your report to the platform and any law enforcement referral.

3. Self-report to the platform before the seller does

This is the step people resist, and it is usually the single most protective action available.

Platforms distinguish sharply between a business that discloses a violation and cooperates in removing the content, and a business that is caught and denies it. Contact the content-integrity or trust and safety team for each affected platform, state plainly what you bought and from whom, and ask them to remove it.

Two things follow. Penalties are generally assessed more favourably where there is voluntary disclosure and cooperation. And — decisively — it destroys the seller's leverage entirely. A threat to expose you is worthless once you have already disclosed.

Your report is also useful beyond your own case

Coalition members share intelligence on fraudulent operators with one another. Naming the seller, the domain, the payment addresses and the accounts used contributes directly to identifying the network — which is what actually reduces its ability to operate. Buyers who self-report are frequently the best source of intelligence available on a given seller.

4. Report the extortion as a crime

Demanding money under threat of harm is a criminal offence, and it is separate from your own violation. Report it. In the US, to the FBI's IC3 and the FTC. In the UK, to Action Fraud and the CMA. In the EU, to your national consumer authority and police. Google also operates a dedicated reporting form for negative review extortion targeting Business Profiles.

Do not let embarrassment about the original purchase prevent this. Extortion networks operate at scale precisely because their victims are structurally reluctant to come forward.

5. Take advice if the sums are material

If the purchase was substantial or a regulator has already made contact, take professional legal advice before responding. Nothing on this page is legal advice.

6. Rebuild the legitimate way

The original problem — too few reviews — still exists. Ask every customer, use the platform's own review-request tools, ask your whole customer base rather than only the customers you expect to be positive, and offer nothing in exchange. It is slower than a purchased package and it is the only version that cannot be taken away from you. There is more on this on the main page.
