I paid for reviews and got scammed. What now?

Last reviewed 30 August 2026

Assume the money is gone. The urgent issue is different: the seller now holds your business details, your payment record and proof that you commissioned an unlawful act. That is leverage, and a meaningful share of sellers use it.

Two things matter most. Stop paying. And self-report to the platform before the seller does.

What is happening to you is the business model

The likeliest situation is one of four: the reviews never arrived; they arrived and were removed; the seller kept escalating — a delivery fee, an unlocking fee, a premium tier — with each payment producing another demand; or the seller has started making threats.

All four are standard. None of them mean you were unlucky with an otherwise legitimate vendor.

Once a seller holds your business link and evidence you paid, you stop being a customer to retain and become a target with a demonstrated willingness to pay and a strong reason not to complain. Some will threaten to report you to the platform or your regulator. Some turn a wave of negative reviews on you instead and demand payment to stop.

Paying does not end it. This is the most consistent finding in reporting on review extortion — businesses pay, and fresh demands and further negative reviews follow. Those who come through it intact almost never pay, because paying identifies you as someone who will pay again.

Why there is almost no recourse

The arrangement is unenforceable. You cannot sue on an agreement to commit an unlawful act. There is no forum in which “I paid for fake reviews and they weren’t delivered” is a claim.

The payment was designed not to be reversible. Sellers choose collection methods for exactly that reason.

And complaining means disclosing. The seller’s real protection is not anonymity — it is that reporting them means reporting yourself. That is deliberate. It is also less of a trap than it looks.

What to do, in order

1. Stop paying, and stop negotiating

Send nothing further and don’t discuss a smaller amount. Every reply confirms the channel is live and the target responsive. Cease contact.

2. Preserve everything

Before deleting anything, screenshot the full message history, the site you bought from, the payment confirmations and any threats. Do it now — seller infrastructure is deliberately short-lived, and this evidence supports both your report to the platform and any referral to law enforcement.

3. Self-report before the seller does

This is the step people resist, and usually the most protective action available. Contact the content-integrity or trust and safety team for each affected platform, state plainly what you bought and from whom, and ask them to remove it.

Platforms distinguish sharply between a business that discloses and cooperates and one that is caught and denies it. Penalties are generally assessed more favourably where there is voluntary disclosure. And decisively: it destroys the seller’s leverage. A threat to expose you is worthless once you have already disclosed.

Your report helps beyond your own case

Coalition members share intelligence on fraudulent operators with one another. Naming the seller and the domain you dealt with contributes directly to identifying the network — which is what actually reduces its ability to operate. Buyers who come forward are often the best source of intelligence available on a given seller.

4. Report the extortion as a crime

Demanding money under threat of harm is a criminal offence, separate from your own violation. In the US, report to the FBI’s IC3 and the FTC. In the UK, to Action Fraud and the CMA. In the EU, to your national consumer authority and police. Major platforms also operate dedicated reporting routes for review extortion.

Don’t let embarrassment about the original purchase stop you. These networks operate at scale precisely because their victims are structurally reluctant to come forward.

5. Take advice if the sums are material

If the purchase was substantial, or a regulator has already been in contact, take professional legal advice before responding. Nothing on this page is legal advice.

6. Rebuild the legitimate way

The original problem — too few reviews — still exists. Ask every customer rather than only the ones you expect to be positive, use your platform’s own review-request tools, and offer nothing in exchange. Slower, and it is the only version nobody can take away from you. More on the main page.
